Knee pain on the bike: where it hurts and what to check first

14 November 2026·7 min read

Dalam Bahasa Indonesia: Nyeri Lutut Saat Bersepeda

The knee is the most common place for cyclists to get overuse pain. Where it hurts is a useful clue, because the usual causes differ from the front of the knee to the back and from the inside to the outside. This is the map fitters work from, with an honest note on how strong the evidence behind it is.

This is not medical advice. Sharp pain, swelling, clicking with pain, or pain that stays when you are off the bike are reasons to see a physiotherapist or doctor before you change anything on the bike.

Front of the knee

Pain around or under the kneecap is the most common complaint. The usual suspect is a saddle that is too low. Modelling studies show the pressure between the kneecap and the thigh bone goes up as the saddle goes down, because a more bent knee is loaded harder through every stroke.

Other things that make it worse: a saddle too far forward, pushing big gears at low cadence, and a sudden jump in how much you ride.

What to check: the knee angle at the bottom of the stroke. On video, more than about 40° of bend points to a low saddle.

Back of the knee

Pain behind the knee usually comes from the leg straightening too far at the bottom of the stroke, which stretches the hamstring tendons and the structures behind the joint. The usual cause is a saddle that is too high, or too far back, which has the same effect. A rider who rocks their hips side to side to reach the pedals is showing the same problem.

What to check: the knee angle again. On video, less than about 28° means the leg is nearly straight.

Outside of the knee

Pain on the outer side, often described as sharp or burning, is commonly linked to the iliotibial band. On the bike it is most often traced to foot position: cleats rotated so the heel points too far in, or a stance that is too narrow. A saddle that is too high can contribute.

What to check: cleat rotation first. Your foot should sit at the angle it naturally wants to, and most pedal systems give a few degrees of float for this.

Inside of the knee

Pain on the inner side is more often linked to cleats rotated with the heel too far out, or to the knee dropping inward through the power phase. That inward drop is what the analyzer's front and rear view tries to read, though that view is still in beta.

What to check: cleat rotation and stance width, then film from the front to see how the knee tracks.

How good is this map?

Good enough to tell you where to look, not good enough to diagnose. A review of overuse injuries in cycling found no strong evidence tying a specific pain to a specific fit measurement. The front-and-low, back-and-high patterns come from biomechanical modelling and from fitters seeing the same thing over and over, not from controlled trials. Pain can also come from things the bike cannot fix, like how much you have been riding lately or how strong your hips are.

The order to work in

  1. Training load. Did the pain start after a big week, a long event, or a return from a break? If so, ease off before touching the bike.
  2. Saddle height. It matters most and it is free to change. Film, check the knee angle, and move in 2–3mm steps.
  3. Cleats. Rotation, then stance width, then fore-aft position.
  4. Saddle fore-aft. Only after height and cleats are settled, because both change how far forward you sit.

Change one thing, ride it for a few sessions, and see what happens before changing the next. If two or three careful changes do not help, a professional fit and a physio are worth the money.
