Bike frame size by height, and why the chart is often wrong

14 November 2026·6 min read

Dalam Bahasa Indonesia: Ukuran Sepeda Sesuai Tinggi Badan

A chart that turns your height into a frame size is a fair starting point, and only that. What matters more is the length of your legs and the length of your torso, and two riders of exactly the same height can need different frames. Here is why the chart is right for some people and off by a size for others.

A rough guide, with every caveat

Road frames are sized in centimetres (the seat tube length) or in letters. Typical ranges:

Your heightRoad frameLetter size
155–165cm48–50XS / S
165–172cm51–53S
172–178cm54–55M
178–185cm56–58L
185–192cm58–60XL

Now the caveat: every brand measures differently. One brand's 54 can ride like another's 56. Modern frames no longer share one geometry, and many brands moved to S/M/L because the centimetre number had stopped meaning much. Never order from the number alone without looking at that brand's geometry chart.

Why height is the wrong measurement

Height is the sum of two things a bike treats very differently: leg length and torso plus arm length.

Two riders at 175cm can be built very differently. One has long legs and a short torso; the other the reverse. The first needs a taller seatpost and a shorter reach to the bars. The second needs the opposite. A chart that only asks your height knows nothing about this.

If you can only measure one thing, measure your inseam.1 Stand barefoot with your back to a wall, pull a book up firmly between your legs, and measure from the floor to the top of the book. It tells you more than your height does, and it is also what saddle height formulas start from.

What you can fix later, and what you cannot

This is the part that matters most when choosing, and the part most often skipped.

Fixable after you buy: saddle height, saddle fore-aft, handlebar height (spacers and stem angle), stem length, bar width, cleat position. All cheap, some free.

Not fixable: how long the frame really is, described by its reach and stack. Reach is the horizontal distance from the bottom bracket to the top of the head tube; stack is the vertical one. If the frame is too long for you, even the shortest sensible stem will not save it: the bars will always feel too far away, and your back pays for it. Too short, and you end up on a very long stem with a bike that steers oddly.

So if you are between two sizes, the question is not which is closer. It is which one's mistake you can fix. Usually that is the smaller size, made up with stem and seatpost, especially if your flexibility is not great yet.

Physical checks still worth doing

Standover. Straddle the bike with your feet flat. There should be clearance between you and the top tube. This is a safety check, not a sizing method: modern sloping top tubes nearly always pass, so passing does not mean the size is right.

Ride it if you can. Five minutes on the bike tells you more than an hour with a geometry chart. If you are buying online, find someone of similar height and build who rides that model.

Gravel, MTB and folding bikes

Gravel bikes mostly use the same sizing as road, with slightly more relaxed geometry. Mountain bikes have almost all moved to S/M/L and are sized more by reach than seat tube. Folding bikes usually come in one size with a wide adjustment range, so the fit comes down to saddle and bar height.

Once the bike arrives

Frame size is a decision you make once. The fit is what you keep adjusting, and it decides whether the bike is comfortable. Start with saddle height, then the reach, and check both from a side-on video.
