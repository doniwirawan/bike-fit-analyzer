Cleat position: the three adjustments and how to set them

14 November 2026·6 min read

Dalam Bahasa Indonesia: Posisi Cleat Sepeda yang Benar

A cleat adjusts three ways: forward and back, rotation, and side to side. Most riders have only ever touched one of them. It is also the part of a fit people skip most often, even though it sits exactly where all your power goes into the bike.

Why it matters more than it looks

Your foot is locked to the pedal. If that lock holds your foot at an angle that is not natural for you, the knee has no choice but to twist with it, around five thousand times an hour.

That is why cleat problems are usually felt in the knee, not the foot.1 People then spend weeks adjusting the saddle for a problem that sits twenty centimetres lower down.

1. Forward and back

The adjustment with the most effect, and the one people talk about most.

Starting point: the bony bump at the base of your big toe (the first metatarsal head) directly over the pedal axle, or slightly behind it. To check, feel for the bump through the shoe, mark it on the side of the shoe, fit the cleat, and look from the side to see whether the mark lines up with the axle.2

Moving the cleat back, so more of your foot sits ahead of the axle, takes load off the calf and Achilles tendon. Many long-distance riders like it. Moving it forward feels snappier for sprinting. Most riders are fine somewhere in between.

One catch: moving the cleat back effectively lowers your saddle, because the distance from your sit bones to the point you push through changes. If you move the cleat more than a few millimetres, check your knee angle again.

2. Rotation

The one most often wrong, and the one most directly tied to knee pain.

The rule: the cleat should follow the angle your foot naturally takes, not force it straight ahead. Some people's toes point slightly out when they walk, some slightly in. Both are normal, and the cleat should respect either.

A simple check: sit on the edge of a table with your legs hanging loose and relaxed, and see which way your toes point on their own. That is the angle to set.

Mind the float too, the few degrees a cleat lets your foot turn before it releases. A zero-float cleat holds your foot at one fixed angle and only makes sense if that angle is exactly right. For most riders, a cleat with a few degrees of float is much kinder to the knees.

3. Side to side

The adjustment almost nobody touches. Moving the cleat towards the inside edge of the shoe puts your foot further from the bike; moving it outwards brings it closer. The total distance between your feet is the Q-factor, and ideally it suits the width of your hips.

A rough guide: if your knees drift inward as you pedal, try moving your feet further from the bike. If they bow outward, the opposite. For bigger changes there are pedals with longer axles and dedicated spacers.

How to change them without losing your way

  1. Mark the old position first. Trace around the cleat on the sole with a marker before you loosen anything. It is your way back.
  2. Change one adjustment at a time, and only a little. Cleats are sensitive. A change that feels trivial in your hand can feel large in your knee after an hour.
  3. Test on a short, safe ride first. A hundred-kilometre ride is not the place to try new cleats.
  4. Tighten to the specified torque. A loose cleat bolt lets the cleat turn as you pedal, and you end up chasing a problem that keeps moving.
  5. Check your knee angle again after moving the cleat forward or back more than a few millimetres, or after changing shoes.

What video can and cannot show

This is the part of a fit our analyzer helps with least. It reads your position from the side, and cleat rotation and Q-factor happen in a plane you cannot see from there. Even from the front, judging knee movement on 2D video has a floor: the smallest difference it can reliably detect is around 7.5–9°,3 so fine cleat changes fall inside the measurement noise.

So the analyzer can tell you your saddle is too low. For cleats, the best it can offer is a sensible starting point and advice to change things in small steps. If your knee still hurts after the saddle height is right, cleats are the next suspect, and that is exactly the case where a professional fitter earns their fee.
