Road, gravel, MTB, TT: how the fit changes with the bike

14 November 2026·6 min read

Dalam Bahasa Indonesia: Bike Fit untuk Road, Gravel, dan MTB

People often ask whether a mountain bike needs a different fit from a road bike. Part of the answer is no: your legs do the same job on any bike, so the saddle height rule does not change. The other part is yes: how far forward and how low you lean depends on what the bike is for. Here is how that splits.

What stays the same

Saddle height, measured as the knee angle. At the bottom of the stroke your knee should have about the same bend whatever you ride: 30–40° on video. Your leg does not know what frame it is on. The saddle height number in millimetres will differ between bikes, because cranks, pedals and frames differ, but the knee angle you are aiming for does not.

Soft elbows. Locked arms send every bump straight into your shoulders, on any bike.

One exception for mountain bikes: the knee angle applies with the saddle at full pedalling height. A dropper post lowered for descending is a different position on purpose, and the analyzer should only see the climbing and pedalling position.

What changes: how far you lean

The torso angle, measured from horizontal, is where bikes differ. A lower torso is more aerodynamic and puts more of your weight on your hands. A taller one is more comfortable, easier on the neck and lower back, and gives you a better view and more control off-road.

Bike typeTorso targetWhy
Time trial / triathlon12–28°Speed against the wind is the whole point
Road, race32–42°Aero, for riders with the flexibility for it
Road, endurance40–50°Long days, still efficient
Gravel42–52°Control on loose ground, long hours
Mountain (MTB)46–58°Weight back, eyes up, room to move
City / hybrid / e-bike55–70°Comfort and a view of traffic

These ranges are a fitter's rule of thumb, not a research result. Unlike the knee angle, there is no study that says what torso angle a gravel rider should hold. Treat them as typical, and let comfort decide where you sit inside them, or just outside.

Bike by bike

Time trial and triathlon

The lowest torso of any bike. As the torso drops, the angle between your torso and thigh closes at the top of the stroke, which can cost power and comfort. TT riders usually sit further forward on the saddle to open that angle back up. A low TT position you cannot hold for the length of the race is slower than a slightly higher one you can.

Road

The analyzer splits road into race and endurance because the two positions really are different. If you are new, or riding long, start in the endurance range and only go lower once it feels easy.

Gravel

A touch taller than road endurance. You spend more time steering around things, and the hours on rough surfaces add up in your neck and hands.

Mountain bike

Taller again, with wide bars and a short stem. Reach on a mountain bike is about control as much as comfort, so do not shorten or lengthen it based on one clip. Film on a trainer for a clean side view: trail footage moves too much to measure.

City, hybrid and e-bike

The most upright. If you ride in traffic, being able to look around matters more than any efficiency you would gain by leaning.

Picking the right type in the analyzer

Choose what the bike is used for, not what it was sold as. A road bike with a high front end used for commuting is closer to city. A gravel bike set up low for racing is closer to road race. Only the torso target changes when you switch, so you can try two types on the same clip and see which grade fits how you ride.
