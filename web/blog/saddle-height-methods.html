Saddle height methods compared: heel, 109%, LeMond and the knee angle

14 November 2026·7 min read

Dalam Bahasa Indonesia: Tinggi Sadel Sepeda yang Benar

Ask five riders how they set their saddle height and you will hear five methods. Most of them are formulas built on your inseam. One of them measures the thing that actually matters, which is how bent your knee is at the bottom of the stroke. Here is what each method does, and what happened when researchers checked them against each other.

The four methods

The heel method

Put your heel on the pedal, pedal backwards, and raise the saddle until your leg is just straight at the bottom without your hips rocking. When you clip in properly, with the ball of the foot over the pedal, the knee ends up slightly bent. It needs no tape measure, which is why it is the method most people learn first.

109% of inseam (Hamley and Thomas)

Measure your inseam, crotch to floor, barefoot. Multiply by 1.09. That number is the distance from the pedal spindle, with the crank at the bottom, to the top of the saddle.

LeMond's 0.883

Same inseam, multiplied by 0.883. This time the result is the distance from the centre of the bottom bracket to the top of the saddle, measured along the seat tube. The formula leaves out crank length, so two riders with the same inseam on different cranks get the same number even though their knees will not bend the same amount.

The knee angle (Holmes method)

Instead of predicting the right height from leg length, measure the result: the knee angle with the pedal at the bottom of the stroke. The classic static target, taken with a goniometer while the rider holds still, is 25–35° of knee flexion.

What the research found

A study of 19 trained cyclists set saddle height with the LeMond, heel and 109% methods and then measured the knee angle each one produced. The 109% and LeMond heights were statistically the same as each other, and the heel method came out significantly lower. More to the point, none of the formulas reliably put the knee inside the 25–35° range: a sizeable share of riders landed outside it whichever formula they used. Write-ups of the study quote slightly different percentages, but they agree on the conclusion.

A 2011 systematic review of saddle height and knee injury reached the same place from a different direction. Across the studies it pooled, changing saddle height by 5% changed knee joint kinematics by about 35% and knee joint moments by about 16%. Small changes in height make large changes at the knee. Its recommendation was to set saddle height using the knee flexion angle rather than a formula.

The reason the formulas miss is simple. Your inseam is only one of the things that decides how bent your knee is. Foot length, how you angle your foot through the stroke, cleat and shoe stack height, saddle shape and crank length all change it, and none of those appear in the formula.

Static angles and video angles are not the same number

One catch: the 25–35° range was measured on a still rider. When the same rider is filmed pedalling, the knee reads about 8° more bent than the static number. That is why the analyzer grades against 30–40°, not 25–35°. If you compare a video reading with a static target you will conclude your saddle is too low when it is fine.

How to use them together

  1. Use a formula, or the heel method, to get a starting height if the saddle is nowhere near. They are fine for that.
  2. Ride a few minutes to warm up, then film yourself side-on and check the knee angle at the bottom of the stroke. On video, aim for 30–40°.
  3. Move the saddle in 2–3mm steps, not centimetres, and re-film after each change.
  4. Stop when the knee is in range and the ride feels right. If they disagree, how it feels wins, and that is a good moment to see a fitter.

If you change shoes, cleats, pedals or crank length, check again. Each of those moves your foot relative to the pedal axle, and the knee angle moves with it.
