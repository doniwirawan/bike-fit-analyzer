Saddle soreness: what's normal, what isn't, and how to fix it

14 November 2026·6 min read

Dalam Bahasa Indonesia: Pantat Sakit Saat Bersepeda

A dull ache in the first few weeks is normal and goes away. Chafing, pain that stays, and numbness are not normal, and they almost never go away with a softer saddle. This complaint makes more people quit cycling than any other, and it is the one least often talked about plainly.

This is about setup and kit, not medical advice. Numbness that keeps coming back, or sores that will not heal, are for a doctor.

What's normal

If you are new, or coming back after a long break, the tissue you sit on needs time to adapt. A dull ache after riding that fades within a day and gets lighter every week is part of that. A few weeks of regular riding is usually enough.

What helps in this phase: shorter rides more often, rather than one long ride a week.

What isn't

These are not adaptation, and riding through them will not make them go away:

  • Sharp pain, or pain in one specific spot.
  • Chafing, broken skin or small boils where you sit.
  • Numbness or tingling, especially in the perineum or genitals.
  • Pain that gets worse week by week instead of better.

The last one means something in your setup or kit is wrong. Numbness needs taking more seriously, and has its own section below.

A softer saddle is not the answer

It is almost everyone's first mistake. A very soft saddle feels great for five minutes and becomes the problem after an hour. Thick foam compresses and spreads out, which moves the pressure off your sit bones, which are built to carry weight, and onto the soft tissue around them, which is not.

What matters is not softness but a saddle width that matches the distance between your sit bones.

Measuring your sit bones at home

You need a piece of corrugated cardboard. Put it on a hard, flat chair, sit on it with your feet slightly raised and your body leaning forward as if on the bike, then stand up. You will see two dents. Measure between the deepest points.

A saddle that suits you is usually a few centimetres wider than that. Many makers publish their own conversion, and some shops have a dedicated measuring pad. Too narrow and your sit bones hang off the edges; too wide and it rubs the inside of your thighs.

Four other causes, in order

1. The saddle is too high. If you have to reach for the pedal at the bottom of the stroke, your hips rock a little with every pedal stroke. That rubbing is what causes chafing, and no saddle fixes it. Check your knee angle first: here is how.

2. Saddle tilt. Start level, measured with a spirit level or a level app on your phone. A nose tilted up presses into soft tissue; tilted down, you slide forward and lean on your hands. One or two degrees is a change you will feel.

3. Shorts. A decent pair of padded cycling shorts changes more than a saddle that costs three times as much. And the part that surprises people: you wear them without underwear. Underwear seams sit in exactly the wrong place and hold moisture. For rides over an hour, chamois cream helps too.

4. Hygiene. Wash your shorts after every ride, and do not sit around in damp shorts afterwards. Most sores and boils come from skipping this, not from the saddle.

Numbness is different

Numbness or tingling in the perineum is not a stronger version of an ache. It means pressure on nerves and blood vessels, and ignoring it for months is not wise.

Things to try: lower the saddle nose by a degree or two, make sure the saddle is not too high, stand up out of the saddle every few minutes, and consider a saddle with a cut-out or channel down the middle, which is designed to take pressure off that area. If the numbness keeps coming back, or stays after you get off the bike, see a doctor. It is not part of getting used to it.

The order to work in

  1. Get the saddle height right first. Many saddle complaints are really saddle height complaints.
  2. Level the saddle, then adjust one degree at a time if needed.
  3. Buy decent padded shorts, and wear them without underwear.
  4. Only then measure your sit bones and think about a new saddle.
  5. If sores keep coming back or you get numb, stop guessing: a fitter for the setup, a doctor for the skin and nerves.

Saddles are very personal. The one your friend swears by may be wrong for you, and the reverse. Many shops run test-saddle schemes, and if one is near you, that beats guessing from reviews.
