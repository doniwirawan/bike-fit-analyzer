Back and neck pain on the bike: usually it's the reach

14 November 2026·7 min read

Dalam Bahasa Indonesia: Sakit Punggung dan Leher Saat Bersepeda

If your lower back and neck start complaining an hour into a ride, the reach to the handlebars is usually the first suspect: too far, too low, or both. The second suspect is your own core strength, and no hex key fixes that. Most people fix the wrong end first and buy a new saddle for a problem at the front of the bike.

This is not medical advice. Back pain that stays, spreads down a leg, or comes with numbness needs a physiotherapist or doctor, not a spacer swap.

The three angles that show it

Once the saddle is right, these three readings tell you about the front of the bike. They are what the analyzer measures from your clip. The targets below are for an endurance road position.

  • Torso angle from horizontal: 40–50°. Below 40° you are folded low. That is aerodynamic, and expensive if your back is not ready for it.
  • Elbow bend: 15–30°. Locked straight elbows mean you are reaching. Every bump in the road travels up your arms into your shoulders and neck with nothing to soak it up.
  • Shoulder angle: 80–95°. The angle between your torso and upper arm. If this is the one that is out while the other two look fine, stem length is usually the issue.

Two notes before you compare your numbers with anyone else's. The torso target depends on the bike: road race, gravel, MTB and TT all have their own range. And these ranges are for video of you pedalling. Guides that quote static numbers will read several degrees smaller for the same position.

Signs your reach is too long

  • Your elbows are locked straight most of the time.
  • You crane your neck up to see the road ahead. This is the most direct cause of neck pain.
  • You find yourself perched on the nose of the saddle, because it shortens the reach.
  • Your shoulders are tight after half an hour, not after two.
  • Your lower back burns or aches on long flat roads.
  • Your hands are holding you up, not just holding on.

The cheap fixes first

In order from free to paid. Do one at a time and ride a few times between changes.

  1. Saddle position first, always. A saddle set too far back adds reach without you touching the bars. Sort out the back of the bike before the front.
  2. Raise the bars. Move spacers from above the stem to below it, or flip the stem to point up. Free, ten minutes, and the biggest effect for the effort.
  3. Roll the bars. A few degrees of drop-bar rotation moves the hoods, and the hoods are where your hands spend most of the ride.
  4. Move the hoods along the bar. A few millimetres is often enough.
  5. Then change the stem. 10mm shorter is a change you will clearly feel. It is the cheapest part you might need to buy, and almost always a better answer than a new saddle or handlebar.

A new frame is the last answer, not the first. A frame that is much too big cannot be fully fixed, but far more people buy a new frame when the stem was the problem.

The part that is not the bike's fault

A leaned-over position is held up by your abs, lower back and hips, not your hands. If those muscles are not yet strong enough, your body hands the load to your arms and neck, and your neck complains. A perfectly set bike cannot replace that.

Hamstring and hip flexibility also decide how low is sensible for you today. Pros ride with very low torsos because they have trained for it for years. Copying the position without the years is a quick way to hurt your back. Raise the bars now, and lower them again slowly if your body catches up.

Numb hands are a different problem

Numbness or tingling in your hands or fingers is not a stronger version of an ache. It means pressure on a nerve, and ignoring it for weeks is a bad idea. Take weight off your hands by shortening the reach, change the angle of your wrists, try thicker bar tape or padded gloves, and move your hands around often. If the numbness keeps coming back, or stays after you get off the bike, see a doctor.
